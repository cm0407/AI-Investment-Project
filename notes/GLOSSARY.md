# The Beginner's Investing Glossary

Every term your INVEST system might use — explained simply.

# How to use this
Whenever your daily digest or a research report uses a word you don't know, look it up here. The terms are grouped by topic, not alphabetically, so related ideas sit together and make more sense. You do not need to memorize any of this — just refer back when you need to.

> The five terms that matter most on day one If you only learn five things to start: a Stock is a slice of a company you own; a Ticker is its short code; Valuation asks 'is the price fair?'; a Catalyst is an event that could move the price; and Diversification (not putting all your eggs in one basket) is the closest thing investing has to a free lunch.

# The absolute basics

| Term | What it means (plain English) |
| --- | --- |
| Stock (share) | A small piece of ownership in a company. Own one share of a company with a million shares, and you own one-millionth of it. |
| Ticker | The short letter-code that identifies a stock. Apple is AAPL, Microsoft is MSFT. You use it to look a stock up. |
| Share price | What one share costs right now. On its own it tells you almost nothing about whether a stock is cheap or expensive — you need valuation for that. |
| Market capitalization (market cap) | The total value of a company: share price × number of shares. This is the real 'size' of a company, not its share price. Large-cap = big & established; small-cap = smaller & riskier. |
| Dividend | A cash payment some companies send shareholders, usually quarterly — your slice of the profits. Not all companies pay one. |
| Portfolio | All the investments you own, taken together. |
| Position | A single holding within your portfolio (e.g., 'a position in Apple'). 'Position size' = how much of your money is in that one holding. |
| Volatility | How much a price bounces around. High volatility = big swings up and down. It's a measure of turbulence, not of good or bad. |
| Bull / Bear | Bullish = expecting prices to rise; bearish = expecting them to fall. A 'bull market' is broadly rising; a 'bear market' is broadly falling. |

# Is it a good business?

| Term | What it means (plain English) |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue (sales, 'the top line') | The total money a company brings in before any costs. Growing revenue means the business is selling more. |
| Earnings (net income, profit, 'the bottom line') | What's left after all costs, taxes, and expenses. This is actual profit. |
| EPS (earnings per share) | Profit divided by the number of shares — the profit attributable to each share you own. Rising EPS is usually good. |
| Margin | What portion of sales becomes profit. If a company keeps 20¢ of profit from every $1 of sales, that's a 20% margin. 'Margin expansion' means it's getting more profitable per sale. |
| Gross / Operating / Net margin | Three profit checkpoints: gross (after the direct cost of making the product), operating (after running the business), net (after absolutely everything). Higher is better. |
| Free cash flow (FCF) | The real cash left over after a company pays its bills AND reinvests to keep running. Harder to fake than 'earnings' — many pros trust it most. |
| Moat | A durable advantage that protects a company from competitors — a strong brand, a network, patents, or lower costs. A wide moat helps a company stay profitable for years. |
| Balance sheet strength | Whether a company has more cash than debt and can survive tough times. Strong balance sheet = safer. |
| ROE / ROIC (return on equity / invested capital) | How efficiently a company turns the money it has into profit. Consistently high numbers signal a quality business. |
| Dilution / Buyback | Dilution = the company issues new shares, making each existing share worth a bit less of the pie (usually a negative). A buyback is the opposite — the company buys back its own shares, giving you a bigger slice (usually a positive). |

# Is the price fair? (Valuation)
Valuation is the art of judging whether a stock is cheap, fair, or expensive relative to what the business actually produces. No single number decides it — good analysis uses several.

| Term | What it means (plain English) |
| --- | --- |
| Valuation | The overall judgment of whether a stock's price is reasonable for the business behind it. |
| P/E ratio (price-to-earnings) | Share price ÷ earnings per share. Roughly, how many dollars you pay for each $1 of yearly profit. A high P/E means investors expect strong growth (or the stock is pricey). |
| Forward P/E | The same idea, but using next year's expected earnings instead of last year's actual ones. |
| PEG ratio | P/E adjusted for growth. It helps answer 'is this high P/E justified by how fast the company is growing?' Around 1 is often considered reasonable. |
| EV/EBITDA | A valuation measure that accounts for a company's debt and cash. Useful for comparing companies with different debt levels. |
| P/S and P/FCF | Price compared to sales, or to free cash flow. Handy alternatives when a company has little or no profit yet. |
| DCF (discounted cash flow) | A method that estimates what a company is worth today based on the cash it's expected to generate in the future. Powerful but only as good as its assumptions. |
| Intrinsic value | An estimate of what a company is 'really' worth, independent of today's mood-driven market price. Buying below intrinsic value is the goal. |
| Margin of safety | Buying at a low enough price that you're protected even if you're somewhat wrong. A cushion against mistakes and bad luck. |
| Overvalued / Undervalued | Priced higher than the business seems to justify (over) or lower (under). Undervalued + good business is the classic setup investors hunt for. |

# What could move the stock?

| Term | What it means (plain English) |
| --- | --- |
| Catalyst | A specific upcoming event that could push a stock up or down — a new product, an earnings report, a big contract, a regulatory decision. |
| Earnings report | A company's quarterly financial update. Stocks often jump or drop sharply right after, depending on whether results beat or missed expectations. |
| Guidance | Management's own forecast for future sales or profit. Markets react strongly when guidance is raised or cut. |
| Analyst / Price target | A professional's published guess for where a stock's price is headed. Treat these as opinions, not facts — your system deliberately does NOT confuse them with real value. |
| Insider buying / selling | When a company's own executives buy or sell its shares. Heavy insider buying can be an encouraging sign; heavy selling can be a caution flag. |
| Institutional ownership | How much of a stock is held by big investors like funds. Rising institutional ownership can signal growing professional confidence. |
| Cyclical | A business whose fortunes rise and fall with the broader economy (e.g., carmakers, airlines). Great in booms, painful in downturns. |

# Risk, probability & your system's own words

| Term | What it means (plain English) |
| --- | --- |
| Downside / Downside risk | How much you could realistically lose if things go wrong. Your system always shows this next to the upside — never trust an idea that hides it. |
| Risk/reward ratio | How much you might gain compared to how much you might lose. You want potential reward to comfortably outweigh potential loss. |
| Bear / Base / Bull case | Three scenarios your system builds for each stock: the pessimistic outcome (bear), the most likely (base), and the optimistic one (bull). Seeing all three keeps you honest. |
| Probability estimate | A rough percentage chance your system assigns to an outcome (e.g., '~40% chance of hitting +20%'). It's an informed estimate, not a fact. |
| Conviction / Score (0–100) | How strongly your system rates an idea after its full checklist. 80+ is strong; below 60 gets rejected. It won't inflate scores to make an idea look better. |
| BUY / WATCH / AVOID | Your system's suggested action. WATCH means 'interesting, but wait for more evidence.' None of these are commands — they're researched opinions for you to weigh. |
| Thesis | The core argument for why a stock is a good (or bad) investment — the story plus the evidence behind it. 'Thesis-breaking risk' is the thing that would prove the story wrong. |
| Diversification | Spreading money across different stocks and sectors so one bad pick can't sink you. The closest thing to a free lunch in investing. |
| Core vs. satellite | A common beginner-safe approach: keep the bulk of your money in a boring, diversified 'core' (like index funds), and use a small 'satellite' slice for individual stock picks like the ones this system finds. |
| Index fund | A single investment that holds a whole slice of the market at once (e.g., the 500 biggest U.S. companies). Cheap, diversified, and historically hard for stock-pickers to beat over the long run. |
| Not financial advice | A phrase your system repeats on purpose. It means: this is research and education to help YOU decide — it is not a professional recommendation tailored to your finances. |

End of Glossary. When your digest uses a word that's not here, just ask Claude: 'Explain that like I'm new to investing.'
