## Part 1 — Correcting yesterday's email

### What I claimed

> *"All ten failed the test. Every single scenario range is narrower than a normal year."*

### What's true

**Four of ten.** NICE, Intuit, Amphenol and Ulta.

I got there by running a quick calculation to test something I already believed, picking a
threshold that felt about right, and not going back to justify it once the answer confirmed what
I expected. **A result that agreed with me got less scrutiny than one that disagreed would
have.** That's the error, and it's worth more than the number itself.

### The sharper problem that survives proper measurement

I measured each bear and bull case in "sigmas" — how far it sits from normal in terms of how
much that stock actually moves.

| | Average | |
|---|---|---|
| My **bear** cases | **−0.85 sigma** | 9 of 10 sit *inside* a normal year |
| My **bull** cases | **+1.16 sigma** | 8 of 10 reach *beyond* a normal year |

**I stretch the upside 36% further than the downside. In ten cases out of ten.**

Not one bear case I wrote reaches even −1.1 sigma, while bull cases run to +1.42.

**Why this is worse than what I originally reported.** A range that's too narrow is a
measurement mistake. A range that's *lopsided* is a **disposition** — it means the downside in
every scenario I've written for you is thinner than the upside, consistently, in the one
direction that hurts a beginner.

**This is now enforced in code**, not just written down. Every scenario set gets checked against
the stock's own volatility before it reaches you.

I screened a fresh batch I'd never examined — deliberately weighted toward businesses whose

### Badger Meter — the one that came closest

Water meters sold to municipal utilities. Demand comes from regulated replacement cycles and
water-loss rules, not from anyone feeling optimistic — exactly the right *kind* of business
right now. Revenue growing 16.6%, 20% operating margins, no dilution.

**It failed on price.** At $151 the market requires **18.1% annual growth** from a company
delivering **16.1%**. You'd be paying for more than it produces.

And here's where yesterday's fix earned its keep: Badger Meter swings **41.7%** in a typical
year. Before this week I'd have written a "−28% bear case." Properly sized, it's **−52%**.
Nearly doubling the loss the position must survive is what pushed its risk/reward score down
and the total below the bar. **A rule that never changes an outcome isn't being applied.** This
one changed a verdict on first use.

| **McDonald's** | 22.4% a year | 9.2% |
| **Rollins** (pest control) | 19.1% | 9.5% |

Alphabet (29.6× earnings), Microsoft (29.1×), Cintas (44.5×) — all good businesses, all

**Note the irony.** McDonald's and Rollins are precisely the defensive businesses that hold up

### And one that was a trap

**Freshworks topped the screen at 89%** on a 21.9% net margin. Its **operating** margin is
**1.6%**.

Net income $184M; operating income $13M — after four straight years of operating losses. The
profit came from below the operating line, which means a one-off event, not the business earning
money. My screen scored the net margin without checking the operating margin beside it.

Fixed. That's the **third** time this exact shape of error has appeared (Harmony in September,
HEICO in September, now Freshworks) — a headline number scored without cross-checking the line
next to it. The real fix isn't another rule: **for every metric I rank on, I now name the figure
that would contradict it and check that too.**

## Jargon I used

- **Sigma / standard deviation** — a measure of normal variation. A "1-sigma" year is an
  ordinary one for that stock; a genuine bear case should be worse than ordinary.
- **Operating margin vs net margin** — operating margin is profit from *running the business*.
  Net margin includes everything else, including one-offs. When net is much higher than
  operating, the difference usually doesn't repeat.
- **Reverse DCF** — asks what growth today's price already assumes, instead of guessing the
  future. If the market demands more than the company has ever delivered, you're overpaying.
- **Credit spread** — extra interest risky borrowers pay. Widening means lenders are nervous.
- **Non-discretionary** — spending customers can't easily postpone (pest control, water meters,
  medicine). Holds up better when the economy weakens.
