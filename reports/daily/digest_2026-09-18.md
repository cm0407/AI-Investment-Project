## Part 2 — The new research

### Idea #1 — Neurocrine Biosciences (ticker: NBIX) — the only new idea that qualified

**In one sentence:** A drug company with two growing products — one treating involuntary
movements caused by psychiatric medication, the other a rare hormone disorder where it has no
direct competitor.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** Revenue, operating profit **and** net profit rose in every one of
the last four years — revenue $1.49B → $2.86B, profit $0.15B → $0.48B. And it's *speeding up*:
first-quarter 2026 product sales were **$811M, up 44%** on the year before.

At 32 times earnings it isn't cheap, and I want to be straight about that. But here's the test
I lean on — the **reverse DCF**, which asks *what growth does today's price already assume?*
For Neurocrine the answer is **12.7% a year**. It has been delivering **28%**. The price needs
roughly half of what the company has been doing.

**What could go wrong:** The older drug, INGREZZA, still brings in most of the money. All the
excitement is about the *smaller* product. If INGREZZA hits pricing pressure — a live political
issue for expensive specialty drugs — that damages the majority of revenue at once, and the
newer product can't fill the hole quickly.

Also: a drug patent is **a legal monopoly with an expiry date printed on it**. The new product's
monopoly is real today, but a competitor is expected within about three years. And the company
**issued 4.8% more shares** over the period — meaning your slice of it shrank slightly.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+17%**. If it goes badly → **~−30%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~70%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~25%**.

**Score: 71.8/100 · Suggested action: WATCH**

⚠️ **One portfolio note:** you already have Exelixis open from 11 September. Both are drug
companies whose value depends on patents and regulatory decisions. Owning both would stack the

### The six I rejected — and why that's the useful part

**Harmony Biosciences (HRMY)** — **my screen ranked this #1 out of 4,354 companies**, at 95.9%,
on a cheap 15.7× earnings and a 14% cash yield.

Then I looked at the profit line. Revenue nearly **doubled** over three years (+98%) while net
profit **fell 13%**. Operating profit was flat for three straight years.

Selling twice as much and earning less is a warning sign, not a growth story. **My screen
didn't check for it** — it scored revenue growth heavily and never asked whether profit
followed. More on that below.

**United Therapeutics (UTHR)** — this would have been my top pick on the numbers: profits rising
every year, a 46.9% operating margin, and only 16× earnings.

But its annual report describes last year. When I checked what's happening *now*, its main
product's revenue had **fallen 18% year-on-year** as a competitor's drug took share after
launching. The cheap price wasn't a bargain — it was the market pricing a decline my data
couldn't see yet.

**Paychex (PAYX)** — looked like solid growth ($4.91B → $6.30B of revenue). Most of that came
from **buying another company**. Growth generated by the existing business is only **4–5%**,
and next year's guidance is 5–6%.

**Ubiquiti (UI)** — a genuinely superb business (116% return on capital). But at today's price
the market requires **29.5% annual growth for a decade**, and it has delivered **8.9%**. The

**Netflix (NFLX) — 69.7/100** and **Qualys (QLYS) — 68.6/100.** Both good companies, both landed

## Part 3 — What I'm changing

**1. My screen now checks that profit keeps pace with revenue.**
This is the big one. Harmony ranked first *because* my scoring weighted revenue growth at 30%
and never checked whether profit followed. I've added the test — 23 companies dropped out of
the results, Harmony among them.

The uncomfortable detail: **I had already written this lesson down** on 9 September, after
finding the same pattern at Ulta Beauty. I wrote "read the profit line, not the per-share line"
in my notes — and then built a screen that ignored it. A lesson written in prose but not
encoded in the code is a lesson that gets forgotten. Where a rule can be made mechanical, I'll
make it mechanical.

**2. I'll check the current quarter before trusting an annual filing.**
United Therapeutics is the second time this has caught me — EOG was the first, back on
9 September. Both had the same shape: **a cheap multiple attached to excellent past figures,
hiding a business already in decline.** From now on, a low price on a great trailing record
makes me go looking for what's breaking, rather than getting excited.

**3. I'll stop re-judging theses on a few days of price movement.** (The Intuit mistake above.)

**4. A known gap I'm recording rather than pretending to fix:** my screen can't tell organic
growth from growth bought through acquisitions. Paychex passed because of it. That has to be
checked by hand, per company, and I'll note it whenever it matters.

## Jargon I used

- **Reverse DCF** — instead of guessing a company's future to produce a target price, it asks
  what growth today's price already assumes. Then you judge whether that's beatable.
- **Operating profit** — what's left from sales after the costs of actually running the
  business. Rising sales with flat operating profit is a warning sign.
- **Organic growth** — growth from the business you already own, as opposed to growth from
  buying another company. Very different in quality.
- **Multiple / P/E** — price divided by yearly profit. "32 times earnings" means paying $32 for
  each $1 of annual profit.
- **Dilution** — when a company issues new shares, each existing share owns a slightly smaller
  slice of the company.
- **Horizon** — how long I expect a prediction to need. Judging it early is meaningless.
- **Mark to market** — checking what a holding is worth at today's price, without deciding
  whether the original reasoning was right.

Anything else: see `notes/GLOSSARY.md`.
