## Part 2 — PTC: what I got right, and what I got badly wrong

**What I got right.** The business analysis was sound. I identified that its software holds a
manufacturer's entire engineering record — every part, revision and supplier — so leaving means
re-checking decades of data. Almost nobody does. I noted the market was only pricing in **8.6%
growth** from a company that had been delivering **32%**.

**What I got wrong — in my own words, from 11 September:**

> *"the upside is structurally capped. A business growing ARR at 9% does not re-rate
> dramatically... reaching +20% needs the multiple to expand, which I cannot point to a
> specific reason for."*

Target: $152. Actual: **acquired at $205.** I was off by 41 percentage points on the upside.

**Why I was wrong — and this is the useful part.** I scored PTC's catalysts 5 out of 10 because
there was no upcoming *announced event*. But **a genuinely hard-to-replace business trading
below what its own cash generation justifies IS a catalyst** — it makes the company a takeover
target. A buyer like Schneider pays for precisely the stickiness I'd identified.

I spotted that the asset was excellent and cheap, then didn't consider that somebody else might
notice the same thing.

**The thinking error underneath it:** "I cannot point to a catalyst" is a statement about *my
search*. I turned it into "there is no catalyst," which is a statement about *the world*. Those
are different claims, and the gap cost 41 points.

### What this means for the position now

PTC trades at **$194.39** against a **$205 cash offer** expected to close around Q3 2027. The
remaining **5.2%** over nine months is a bet on the deal completing — regulators could block
it, the deal could break. **That is a completely different investment from the one I
recommended**, with different risks. Continuing to hold is a fresh decision, not a continuation.

My original thesis is finished. It worked, for a reason I said wouldn't happen.

## Part 3 — Booking: my bear case is running

Worst performer, down 12.1% and 14.4 points behind the market. Four things happened:

**1. It cut its guidance and raised new debt.** My *base* case assumed Booking "delivers its
guided high-single-digit growth." It didn't — it lowered the bar. That assumption is broken.

**2. App traffic turned negative** — down about 5 percentage points in a quarter to −2.8%
year-on-year, worsening through Q3. My bear case said "room-night growth goes flat or negative."
That is now partially confirmed.

**3. Something I named correctly, and something I missed.** My thesis listed as a structural
threat *"Google increasingly inserting itself between the traveller and the OTA."* What actually
happened: **Meta** launched an AI travel agent and **Expedia** integrated it. I got the
*mechanism* right and the *company* wrong — which means I'd have been monitoring Google and
missed this entirely. Lesson recorded: describe the mechanism, not the likely culprit.

**4. Two things my thesis never anticipated:** oil above $100 a barrel on Middle East tension,
and a **Federal Reserve rate hike**.

Status recorded as **at risk** — the thesis is damaged but not dead. The bear case is running,
not the base case.

## Part 4 — The thing I wasn't watching: rates went up

Reviewing Booking turned up something that affects **everything** on this list.

| | 11 Sep | 12 Oct | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10-year Treasury | 4.83% | **5.22%** | **+39bp** |
| Fed funds rate | 3.63% | 3.75% | **a hike** |
| High-yield credit spread | 2.71% | **3.15%** | +44bp |
| Consumer sentiment | 55.2 | **51.7** | −3.5 |

**Why this matters to you.** When I value a company, I discount its future cash back to today
using a required return — I used 10%. That 10% was chosen against a 4.83% "risk-free" rate (what
you'd earn lending to the US government). The risk-free rate is now **5.22%**.

Keeping the same risk premium means the correct rate today is about **10.4%** — and that
mechanically cuts a DCF-derived fair value by roughly **5.3%**.

**None of my price targets were adjusted for this.** I wrote in September that "none of these
theses need rates to fall," which was correct. But I never built the opposite check: what if
rates *rise*. A macro note that records the weather without an alarm for the weather changing is
a snapshot, not a control.

Also worth noting: **credit spreads widened and consumer sentiment fell.** Both lean specifically
against the economically sensitive positions — Booking and Amphenol.

## Part 5 — What I'm changing

**1. Takeover potential now belongs in the bull case.** For any company that is reasonably sized,
hard to replace, and trading below what its cash generation justifies, being acquired is a real
part of the possible outcomes. I had no slot for it, so I scored it as zero.

**2. I'll stop turning "I can't see it" into "it isn't there."** Absence of evidence in my own
analysis is not an upper bound on reality. No more asserting a ceiling I have no basis for.

**3. Every performance figure comes with the outlier stripped out.** You should always be able
to see whether one position is producing the whole result.

**4. I'll re-check the risk-free rate at every review and say what it does to existing targets.**
Rates moved 39bp against me and I only found out by investigating something else.

**5. Structural risks get described as mechanisms, not companies.** "Anything that intermediates
between the customer and the booking platform" — not "Google."

## Jargon I used

- **Takeover / acquisition** — another company buys this one outright, usually at a premium.
  Normally the biggest single-day gain a stock can have.
- **Merger arbitrage** — once a cash offer exists, the share price sits just below it. The small
  remaining gap is payment for the risk the deal collapses. A bet on paperwork, not business.
- **Risk-free rate** — what you earn lending to the US government. Every other investment is
  judged against it. When it rises, everything else becomes relatively less attractive.
- **Discount rate** — the annual return I demand to make an investment worthwhile. Higher
  discount rate = lower value today.
- **Credit spread** — the extra interest risky borrowers pay. Widening means lenders are getting
  nervous.
- **Guidance** — a company's own forecast. A *cut* means management now expects less than it
  previously said.
- **Outlier** — one unusual result that distorts an average.

Anything else: see `notes/GLOSSARY.md`.
