## Part 2 — The bug worth telling you about

I screened a batch of companies I'd never examined. **HEICO — an aerospace parts maker — came
out #1**, scoring 91.2% with an apparent P/E of **11.6** and a 10.7% cash yield, on 20% revenue
growth. That combination is almost unheard of. It should have made me suspicious, and it did.

**HEICO's official share count on file with the SEC reads 26.9 million shares — dated
15 December 2015.** Eleven years out of date. The real number is 141 million.

Because my system divided by too few shares, it calculated the company as worth **$8 billion
instead of $42 billion**, which made it look five times cheaper than reality.

**Why this particular bug is nasty:** getting the share count too *low* always makes a stock
look *cheap*. So the error doesn't produce random noise — it actively pushes its victims to the
top of a bargain-hunting screen. Exactly where I'd be looking.

**What I'd already got wrong:** I built a detector for this problem on 9 September. It fired
correctly on HEICO. **And my screener used the bad number anyway**, because the detector only
printed a warning instead of blocking the calculation. A guard that spots a bad number but
doesn't stop you using it isn't a guard.

Fixed properly now: if the share count is more than ~13 months stale, the system substitutes the
reliable figure, and if it can't, it refuses to report a valuation at all. HEICO re-scored at
67.5% and is rejected.

## Part 3 — The one new idea

### NICE Ltd (ticker: NICE) — 71.4/100 · WATCH

**In one sentence:** An Israeli software company that runs the phone and chat systems large
companies use for customer service — now priced as though that business is dying.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** The numbers are genuinely good — revenue grew from $2.2B to $2.9B
over three years with profits rising alongside, a 21.9% operating margin, and a **16.2% return
on invested capital** (it earns about 16 cents a year on every dollar put into the business).
It scored on 15 of my 16 measurements, so there's little hidden.

You can buy that for **10.4 times earnings**, with an **11% free-cash-flow yield** — meaning
the business throws off cash equal to 11% of its price each year.

The reverse DCF is striking. At today's price the market expects NICE's cash flow to **shrink
by about 9% a year, every year, for a decade.** It has actually been *growing* at 9%. The price
doesn't assume growth stops — it assumes permanent decline.

**What could go wrong — and this is substantial:** NICE charges per human agent using its
software. **AI agents that answer customer queries directly attack that.** If software handles
the calls, a product priced per person has a structural problem, and smaller customers are
already reported to be choosing cheaper AI alternatives.

Worse, **management has already cut its own profit margin forecast from 31% to 25–26%**, to
fund AI investment after buying a German AI startup. That matters for the "cheap" claim: a
10.4× multiple calculated on 31% margins is *not* 10.4× on 25% margins. The real multiple is
higher than the headline.

And it now competes directly against **Microsoft and Salesforce**, both of which can bundle
similar capability into software customers already buy.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+18%**. If it goes badly → **~−33%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~67%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~22%**.

**Score: 71.4/100 · Suggested action: WATCH**

**This is the third time I've made the same bet.**

Adobe (9 Sep), Intuit (11 Sep) and now NICE all rest on one identical sentence: *"the market is
over-punishing an established software company because of AI fear."*

Each looks reasonable on its own. Together they are **not three ideas — they are one idea held

And of the three, **NICE is arguably the weakest**: it's the only one that has already *cut its

the cheapest, but cheap is what deterioration looks like on the way down.

## Jargon I used

- **Reverse DCF** — rather than guessing a company's future, it asks what growth today's price
  already assumes. Then you judge whether that's beatable.
- **Share count** — how many slices the company is divided into. Price × share count = what the
  whole company is worth. Get the count wrong and every valuation is wrong.
- **Free-cash-flow yield** — the cash a business generates as a percentage of its price. Like an
  interest rate on the business.
- **ROIC** — return on invested capital: the "interest rate" the business earns on money put
  into it. Above ~15% is genuinely good.
- **Per-seat pricing** — charging per person using the software. Vulnerable if AI reduces how
  many people are needed.
- **Benchmark** — a yardstick (here, the S&P 500) showing what you'd have got without picking
  stocks at all.
- **Correlated** — moving together. Three stocks that fail for the same reason aren't three
  separate risks.
