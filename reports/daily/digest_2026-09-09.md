## Idea #1 — Adobe (ticker: ADBE) — the most interesting one

**In one sentence:** The company behind Photoshop and PDF, whose stock has fallen 37% this
year because investors fear AI will destroy its business — leaving an exceptionally
profitable company at a bargain-bin price.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** Adobe earns 36.6 cents of operating profit on every sales dollar,
generates $9.85B of genuine cash a year, and holds more cash than debt. You can buy that for
14.3 times profits — the kind of price normally attached to a dying business. At today's
price the market is assuming Adobe's cash flow grows about **1–2% a year for a decade**. It
has actually been growing at **13%**. If Adobe merely proves it isn't dying, the stock
re-rates. It's also the only one of the four whose growth record isn't distorted by an odd
starting year.

**What could go wrong:** The bears may simply be right. AI tools like Canva and Figma are
reportedly growing 30–40% a year while Adobe has slowed to about 11%. Adobe's whole business
rests on image and design work being *hard*. If AI makes it easy, the value moves from the
tool to the AI model, and no amount of cheapness saves you. Cheap stocks in dying industries
stay cheap for years — that's the classic value trap, and I can't rule it out. This is the
honest reason it's a WATCH and not a BUY.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+18%**. If it goes badly → **~−30%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~70%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~25%**.

**Score: 72.7/100 · Suggested action: WATCH**

**What would change it to BUY:** two quarters in a row where Adobe's subscription growth
stops slowing.

## Idea #2 — Booking Holdings (ticker: BKNG) — the highest score

**In one sentence:** The world's biggest online travel marketplace (Booking.com, Priceline,
Kayak) — a genuinely excellent business, but priced like one and slowing down.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** It owns no hotels, so it needs very little money to grow, and
keeps 32.8 cents of every sales dollar as operating profit. It's returning cash at a record
pace — $4.1B in one quarter, $3.7B of it buying back its own shares, which has shrunk the
share count by 20.7%. Fewer shares means each one you own is worth more.

**What could go wrong:** Travel is one of the first things people cut in a recession — in
2020 revenue fell 60% and the company lost money. You'd be paying 25 times earnings for that
risk, which is a full price with no discount. Management's own guidance is for growth to
*slow* to 4–6%. Two things bothered me enough to mark it down: the company has bought back so
much stock that shareholders' equity is **negative $5.6B**, and across the insider filings I
examined there were **55 sales and zero purchases**. That last one proves nothing by itself —
most executive sales are pre-scheduled — but not one insider bought at any price.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+14%**. If it goes badly → **~−33%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~75%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~25%**.

**Score: 77.8/100 · Suggested action: WATCH**

## Idea #4 — Ulta Beauty (ticker: ULTA) — rejected, and the most instructive

**In one sentence:** America's biggest beauty retailer, which is selling more every year while
*earning less* — and hiding that fact behind share buybacks.

**Why it looked attractive:** Sales grew about 10% last year, management just *raised* its
full-year forecast in August, and it beat expectations last quarter. The balance sheet is
pristine, with almost no debt.

**What could go wrong — this is the part worth reading:** Look at what actually happened over
three years:

| | 3 years ago | 2 years ago | Latest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $11.21B | $11.30B | **$12.39B** ⬆ |
| Operating profit | $1.68B | $1.56B | **$1.53B** ⬇ |
| Net profit | $1.29B | $1.20B | **$1.15B** ⬇ |
| **Earnings per share** | $26.03 | $25.34 | **$25.64** ➡ |

**Sales up. Profits down 11%. But earnings per share look flat.** How? The company bought
back shares, cutting the count from 50 million to 45 million. Divide a smaller profit among
fewer shares and the per-share number holds steady. That's legitimate — but the headline
"EPS growth of 11.9–13.1%" describes arithmetic, not a healthier business. A retailer selling
more while earning less is usually discounting to hold customers.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+12%**. If it goes badly → **~−28%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~65%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~20%**.
Weighing those together gives an expected return of just **+1.6%** — the worst of the four.

**Score: 66.2/100 · Suggested action: AVOID**

## Jargon I used

- **Free cash flow** — the real cash left after paying bills and reinvesting. Harder to fake
  than accounting profit.
- **FCF yield** — that cash as a percentage of the company's price. Like an interest rate on
  the business.
- **P/E ratio** — price divided by yearly profit. How many dollars you pay per $1 of profit.
- **Operating margin** — how much of each sales dollar survives the cost of running the
  business.
- **Buyback** — a company buying its own shares, leaving each remaining share owning more.
- **Comps** — sales growth at stores open at least a year, stripping out new store openings.
- **Value trap** — a stock that looks cheap but keeps getting cheaper because the business is
  genuinely deteriorating.
