## Idea #1 — Intuit (ticker: INTU) — my best idea

**In one sentence:** The company behind TurboTax and QuickBooks, whose shares have fallen ~38%
this year because investors fear AI will make its software unnecessary.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** Of everything I looked at, Intuit was the only company where
revenue, operating profit **and** net profit went up in *every one* of the last five years —
$12.73B to $21.45B of revenue, $2.07B to $4.57B of profit. No accounting quirks, no odd
starting year. You can buy that for **18.3 times earnings**, and it throws off **$8.66B of
real cash** a year — a 10.4% cash return on its price.

Here's the part that made me look twice. I ran a "reverse DCF" — instead of guessing the
future, it asks *what is today's price already assuming?* The answer: the market expects
Intuit's cash to **shrink about 6% a year for a decade**. It has been **growing at 22%**. The
price doesn't assume growth stops. It assumes permanent decline.

**What could go wrong:** The people selling might simply be right. And some of their evidence
comes from Intuit itself — the company **lowered its own TurboTax forecast**, guided consumer
tax growth to just **2–3%**, and the **CEO admitted they "lost quality DIY customers to
low-cost providers this year."** Then they cut **17% of staff**. That's not a rumour; that's
management confirming the first half of the bear case.

The real danger isn't TurboTax, which is already beaten up. It's **QuickBooks** — the
small-business accounting half, which is the more valuable part. If AI can keep books
competently, the thing that keeps customers locked in gets weaker.

I should also flag: **this is the same bet I made on Adobe** two sessions ago — "the market is
wrong about AI killing established software." If I'm wrong, I'm wrong on both.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+15%**. If it goes badly → **~−28%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~70%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~28%**.

**Score: 79.4/100 · Suggested action: WATCH**

⚠️ **Already moved:** $312.77 when I analysed it → **$339.15 now (+8.4%)**. My target was $360,
so only about **6% of upside remains** at today's price.

## Idea #2 — Amphenol (ticker: APH)

**In one sentence:** Makes the connectors and cables inside AI data centres — a
picks-and-shovels supplier to the AI boom, which is both why it looks good and why I'm wary.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** Revenue jumped **52% in one year**, from $15.2B to $23.1B. The
data-centre part of the business grew **89%** and is now 43% of sales. Management guided next
quarter to another 50–52% growth. At 23 times earnings, that doesn't look expensive for that
kind of growth.

**What could go wrong:** That last sentence is the trap, and it's worth understanding because
it catches beginners often. **Cyclical companies look cheapest right at the top.** The
multiple is calculated on boom-year profits that everyone expects to fade. If profits fall 30%
*and* the multiple drops from 23× to 16×, the stock roughly halves. That's how I get to a
**−38% downside** — that's not a disaster scenario, it's just normal cooling-off arithmetic.

Note too that every catalyst I could find is a "the boom continues" catalyst. None of them is
something the company does that creates value on its own.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+16%**. If it goes badly → **~−38%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~67%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~22%**.

**Score: 77.3/100 · Suggested action: WATCH** — and of the five, the one I'd be slowest to buy.
Weighing the outcomes gives just **+3.4%** expected return for the worst downside in the group.

Now $78.58 (−2.1% since analysis).

## Idea #3 — PTC (ticker: PTC) — the most reliable one

**In one sentence:** Sells the software engineers use to design physical products and store
every version of the blueprints — boring, sticky, and hard to leave.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** This is the **stickiest business** I found. Its software holds a

## Idea #4 — Exelixis (ticker: EXEL) — the one with a real date on it

**In one sentence:** A cancer-drug company with one big product and a second one awaiting an
FDA decision on **3 December 2026**.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** This has the clearest **catalyst** — a specific upcoming event that
could move the price — of anything I found. The FDA has a **target decision date of 3 December**
for its new drug, zanzalintinib. And here's the key: the company's own revenue forecast
($2.53–2.63B) **explicitly leaves that drug out**. So an approval is upside that isn't in the
numbers yet.

The existing business is genuinely good: profit went from $0.17B to $0.87B in two years, 37.6%
operating margins, and it has bought back **11.4% of its own shares** — unusual discipline for
a drug company, where issuing new shares is the norm.

**What could go wrong:** Almost everything rests on one drug and one date. **If the FDA says no
on 3 December, this is a one-product company watching its patent clock run down** — sales of
the existing drug are expected to peak around 2029 and then fall as cheap copies arrive.

A drug patent isn't like a strong brand. It's a **legal monopoly with an expiry date printed
on it.** Every year without a replacement, the company is worth less.

**I also have to be straight with you: I can't read the medical evidence.** I'm reasoning from
financial statements and regulatory dates, not oncology. That's a real limit on how much this
opinion is worth.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+15%**. If it goes badly → **~−32%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~68%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~23%**.

**Score: 76.7/100 · Suggested action: WATCH** — the most speculative of the five. This is the
one where bad news arrives *suddenly*, on a single day, rather than gradually.

## Idea #5 — ResMed (ticker: RMD)

**In one sentence:** Makes breathing machines for sleep apnea — an excellent business facing an
awkward question about whether weight-loss drugs make it less necessary.

**Why it might rise 20%+:** Alongside Intuit, the cleanest record in the group — revenue,
operating profit and net profit all rising for five years, 33.4% operating margins, and a
**25.9% return on invested capital** (it earns about 26 cents a year on every dollar put into
the business). The share count is **flat**, so the profit growth is real earning power, not
buyback arithmetic. It's a razor-and-blade model: sell the machine once, sell replacement masks
for years.

**What could go wrong:** Two dated problems, and one of them is the *nearest* catalyst — which
is unusual and bad. ResMed has **suspended sales of its Astral ventilator** after a safety
action, costing a stated **$75M of revenue**. And **Philips is returning to the U.S. market**
it left after a recall — the very absence that let ResMed's profits expand.

The bigger question: **Eli Lilly's weight-loss drug is approved to treat sleep apnea without a
machine at all**, and 70–90% of sufferers have the condition because of excess weight. If a
pill treats the cause, the machine treats a shrinking group. That may take a decade to show up
in revenue — but the *share price* can fall on the fear long before then. That's exactly what's
happening.

**My honest read:** If it goes well → **~+15%**. If it goes badly → **~−28%**.
Chance of a positive return: **~68%**. Chance of hitting +20%: **~22%**.

**Score: 74.6/100 · Suggested action: WATCH** — lowest of the five.

## Jargon I used

- **Free cash flow** — the real cash left after paying bills and reinvesting. Harder to fake
  than reported profit.
- **Reverse DCF** — instead of guessing a company's future to get a target price, it asks what
  growth today's price already assumes. Then you judge whether that's beatable.
- **Multiple / P/E** — price divided by yearly profit. "18 times earnings" means paying $18 for
  each $1 of annual profit.
- **Cyclical** — a business whose fortunes rise and fall with the wider economy. Looks cheapest
  at the top of its cycle, which is the trap.
- **Catalyst** — a *specific, dated* event that could move the price. "Things should improve"
  is not a catalyst.
- **Recurring revenue / ARR** — money customers pay every year automatically. More predictable
  than one-off sales.
- **ROIC** — return on invested capital: the "interest rate" a business earns on money put into
  it. Above ~15% is genuinely good.
- **Value trap** — a stock that looks cheap and keeps getting cheaper because the business is
  actually deteriorating.
