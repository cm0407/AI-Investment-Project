## Problem 1 — The stocks I told you to skip are winning

### EOG — this one is my mistake, clearly

I rejected it writing: *"the return depends chiefly on the oil price, which I have no framework
to forecast."* Then I rated it **AVOID**.

Oil went above $100 a barrel. EOG is up 12.2%, within 2.4% of the target I'd set.

Read what I did there. I was **right** that oil would decide the outcome. Then I turned *my own
inability to forecast oil* into *an expectation that it would do badly*. Those are completely
different statements.

**AVOID is a prediction.** It needs positive evidence that something will go badly — not an
absence of evidence about what will happen at all. The honest answer was **"no opinion — this
is outside what I can assess."**

### ULTA — the opposite caution

Up 13.7%, past my target. But here's the thing: **it hasn't reported earnings since I rejected
it.** Next results are around 3 December.

The problem I rejected it for — operating profit falling three years running while revenue grew,
with per-share earnings propped up by buybacks — is completely **untested**. The stock went up
on no new information about it.

So my analysis isn't shown to be wrong. It's shown to be **slow**. I shouldn't confuse those,
in either direction.

## Problem 2 — My targets describe an ordinary year, not a good one

**Six of eleven positions are at or within 3% of a twelve-month target, after 12% of the time.**
Three have passed it: PTC, Ulta, ResMed.

At the same time, Booking is already **41% of the way to its bear case.**

Both ends arriving that fast told me the problem wasn't my *direction* — it was my *range*. So I
tested it against how much these stocks actually move:

| Stock | How much it typically moves in a year | The range I gave it |
|---|---|---|
| NICE | **±48%** | −33% to +50% |
| INTU | ±42% | −28% to +50% |
| APH | ±41% | −38% to +40% |
| ADBE | ±38% | −30% to +50% |
| BKNG | ±33% | −33% to +38% |
| RMD | ±28% | −28% to +31% |

**All ten failed the test.** Every single scenario range is narrower than a *normal* year for
that stock.

**In plain terms:** NICE routinely swings about 48% in a year. I called −33% its "disaster case"
and +50% its "best case." Those aren't extremes — **that's a Tuesday.**

### Why this matters more than it sounds

The probabilities I attach to those cases are now also wrong. When I tell you *"30% chance of
the bear case,"* that sounds appropriately cautious. But if the "bear case" is really just an
ordinary bad stretch rather than a genuine disaster, then 30% isn't conservative — **it may be
optimistic.** I've been making the whole thing look more precise than it is.

---

## What I'm changing

**1. Scenario ranges sized against actual volatility.** If a stock swings 40% a year, a −30%
bear case is meaningless. It needs to be nearer −50% to represent something that genuinely
hurts.

**2. "Outside my competence" now gets a *no opinion*, not an AVOID.** EOG taught me that.

**3. Check whether the company has actually reported** before reading anything into the price.
Three of my biggest movers — Ulta, ResMed, Amphenol — have released no new results at all. A
price move with no news tells you about sentiment, not about a business.

**4. Keep reporting mean, ex-outlier and median together**, so you can always see whether one
position is doing all the work.

## Jargon I used

- **Volatility** — how much a share price typically swings in a year. A "40% volatility" stock
  routinely moves 40% up or down over twelve months without anything unusual happening.
- **Bear / base / bull case** — the pessimistic, most-likely and optimistic outcomes. The point
  is that the bear case should describe something that genuinely hurts.
- **Median** — the middle value. Unlike an average, one enormous number can't distort it.
- **Pulled-forward orders** — customers buying earlier than they otherwise would, which flatters
  current sales and starves later ones.
- **Calibration** — checking whether your stated confidence matches what actually happens. The
  whole reason predictions get written down before the outcome.
