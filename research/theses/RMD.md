## 1. The business in one paragraph

ResMed makes **CPAP machines and masks** for sleep apnea — a condition where breathing
repeatedly stops during sleep — plus ventilators and software for home health providers. The
model is razor-and-blade: sell a device once, then sell replacement masks, tubes and filters
for years. That resupply stream is recurring and high-margin, and it is why the installed base
matters more than any single year's device sales.

## 3. Valuation

20.8× earnings for a business with 33.4% operating margins, 25.9% ROIC and a recurring
resupply annuity is not obviously expensive. Per-share growth is genuine here rather than
engineered — the share count is flat, so earnings growth is the company actually earning more,
not buying back stock to flatter the per-share line. (Contrast Ulta from the previous session,
where flat EPS masked an 11% fall in net income.)

**But the multiple is being held down deliberately, not accidentally.** Market debate over
GLP-1 weight-loss drugs is explicitly constraining what investors will pay. That is the
central question, and it is not a valuation question — it is a "does this business still exist
in ten years" question.

- Multiple stays constrained by the unresolved GLP-1 debate regardless of reported results.

**Base assumptions:**
- GLP-1s prove to be net demand generators, as management argues — more diagnosed patients entering the funnel offsets some substitution.
- Philips' return is gradual and ResMed defends most of its share.
- The Astral headwind is absorbed within fiscal 2027 and does not recur.
- Steady mid-single-digit revenue growth with the resupply annuity intact; modest multiple expansion.

**Bull assumptions:**
- GLP-1 screening demonstrably expands the diagnosed population and ResMed captures most new patients.
- Software and residential care (AirView, MatrixCare) grow into a larger share of revenue, improving mix quality.
- Philips' re-entry underwhelms and the competitive fear proves overdone.
- Multiple expands as the GLP-1 overhang lifts.

## 5. Catalysts (Phase 4)

**This is the only idea in the session whose nearest dated catalyst is NEGATIVE**, and that
matters enough to state first:

1. **⚠️ Astral ventilator sales suspended** following a field safety action — a stated **$75M
   revenue headwind and roughly $0.15 per-share earnings drag for fiscal 2027.** Dated,
   quantified, and already in motion.
2. **⚠️ Philips preparing to re-enter the U.S. sleep apnea market** after its recall-driven
   absence — the absence that allowed ResMed's share and margins to expand.

**On the positive side:**
3. Management argues GLP-1 drugs are a **demand-generation opportunity**, funnelling more
   diagnosed patients into the ResMed ecosystem. That is plausible and there is some early
   data supporting it — but it is a thesis, not a dated event, and I have scored it as such.

Catalysts scored **4/10**, the lowest of the five ideas, because a negative dated catalyst
outweighs a positive undated one.

## 6. Why this investment could fail (Phase 5)

**The thesis-breaking risk: a pill replaces the machine.** Eli Lilly's GLP-1 has independent
approval to treat obstructive sleep apnea **without** a CPAP machine. Between 70% and 90% of
sufferers have the condition because of excess weight. If weight-loss drugs treat the
underlying cause, the device addresses a shrinking pool of patients.

This is not a distant hypothetical — it is an approved drug, on the market, today.

**Two things could happen, and they have very different timelines.** The revenue impact is
probably slow: adoption takes years, many patients cannot tolerate or afford GLP-1s, and
severe cases still need a device. But **the multiple can compress long before revenue does**,
purely on the fear — which is precisely what is happening now. A stock can fall 30% on a
threat that never fully materialises.

**Philips is coming back.** ResMed's excellent recent margins were earned partly in a market
where its main competitor had withdrawn after a recall. Getting a competitor back that you
have not had to face for years is a real, dated risk to both price and volume.

**The Astral suspension is a live operational problem.** $75M of revenue and $0.15 of EPS is
not fatal, but a safety action is never just a number — it consumes management attention and
raises questions about quality systems.

**Management's tone gives me pause.** The posture that there is "no new competitive threat that
we are worried about" reads as dismissive given an approved pharmaceutical substitute and a
returning competitor. I marked management down a point for it. Confidence is fine;
appearing not to engage with the central question is not.

**My own bias to watch:** it is tempting to treat a high-quality compounder at 20.8× as a safe
choice. It is not safe — it is a good business facing a genuine substitution threat, and the
low multiple is the market telling me so rather than the market being wrong.

**What would prove me wrong, specifically:** two consecutive quarters of declining new patient
starts, or any evidence that mask/resupply revenue (the annuity) is shrinking rather than just
device sales.

## 8. Verdict

**WATCH.** The underlying business is one of the best in the session — clean five-year
compounding, 33.4% operating margins, 25.9% ROIC, flat share count so per-share growth is
honest.

It scores lowest of the five because the two things that decide the next twelve months are
both outside management's control: whether GLP-1 drugs shrink the patient pool, and how hard
Philips comes back. The probability-weighted return of **+4.7%** with a 32% chance of loss does
not pay for that uncertainty.

**For a beginner specifically:** this is a case where a stock can look cheap and stay cheap for
years while the market waits to find out whether a threat is real. Buying it means being
willing to hold through that uncertainty without a resolution date.

**What would move this to BUY:** clear evidence that GLP-1 prescribing *increases* sleep apnea
diagnoses and device starts — management's own thesis, confirmed by data rather than asserted.
